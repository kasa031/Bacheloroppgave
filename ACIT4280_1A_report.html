Introduction

What this report is about

This report answers two questions about Norwegian-facing web sites. First: when a person opens the front page, how much of that page talks to other companies, and in which countries do those other machines appear to sit? Second: when a site uses personal data, has it named a lawful reason under the GDPR,1 and does that reason hold when we test it with an official guidance tool? The two questions are not the same. A newspaper can load many advertising scripts and still have a legal reason to keep a subscriber account. A tax office can have a strong legal reason to hold income data and still fail a consent test for optional statistics cookies. Part 1 measures contact. Part 2 tests lawfulness for one purpose at a time.

The course list has 18 sites (Table 1). We scanned all 18 with Webbkoll.2 From those 18 we chose five sites (Table 1a) and tested one concrete activity on each with the ICO lawful basis tool.3 The numbers are in Tables 2 and 3. The pictures by sector are Figures 2 to 4. The ICO comparison is Table 5.

What the main words mean

Personal data is information that can identify a person, such as a name, an email address, or an online identifier (General Data Protection Regulation, 2016). The GDPR is the main EU/EEA law for that information. Norway is in the EEA, so the GDPR applies here. Processing means collecting, storing, showing or sending that information. It is lawful only if at least one Article 6 basis fits the purpose (General Data Protection Regulation, 2016, art. 6). The bases used here are legal obligation (the law requires it), public task (a public body doing its job), contract (needed to deliver what the person asked for), consent (a free, informed Yes), and legitimate interests (a real organisational need that does not override the person).

A cookie is a small file stored on the device. An internal (first-party) cookie is set by the site the person opened. An external (third-party) cookie is set by someone else, for example an advertiser. Separate EU rules also cover storing or reading information on a device (Cookie Directive, 2009; ePrivacy Directive, 2002).4 That is why a Yes/No banner can be required for analytics cookies even when tax data rest on law, not on consent.

A third-party request is a network call from the open page to another host, such as fonts, ads, or a video player.5 That is not the same as a third-party cookie. A page can call many other hosts and still set zero third-party cookies.

Part 1: How much does each site contact others?

What Webbkoll is

Webbkoll is a free public scanner (5th of July Foundation, n.d.-c). A person types a web address. The tool then opens that page in a clean Chromium browser6 and writes a report: HTTPS,7 cookies, third-party requests, and the server IP8 (5th of July Foundation, n.d.-a, n.d.-b). Figure 1 shows the start page.

Webbkoll does not read the privacy notice. It does not click Yes or No on a cookie banner. It does not decide whether Article 6 is met. It only shows what that one page load contacted.

How we ran the scans

We used the English Webbkoll interface on every service in Table 1. Each result is one live visit with no add-ons, no Do Not Track,9 and no consent click. We copied the scan clock from the result page. We did this twice on 20 August 2026 so we could see whether a later visit looked different. Webbkoll can keep a result for about 24 hours, so two clocks on the same day can still differ. Table 2 is the first visit. We use Table 2 for all rankings. Table 3 is the later visit. We do not average the two tables.

What KeyCDN is, and what a country cell means

KeyCDN IP Location Finder is a look-up page: we paste an IP address, and it returns a likely country and company name (KeyCDN, n.d.).10 We used it in two ways. For "location of server" we looked up the site's own IP. For "list of countries" we looked up the IPs of the other hosts that Webbkoll listed. We then counted unique countries after dropping Norway, because the assignment asks for countries excluding Norway. If KeyCDN returned no country, we wrote n/a.

A country on that list means: this is where the contacted host appeared to sit at look-up time. It is a map of machines, not a legal file of personal-data exports. GDPR Chapter V sets extra rules when personal data are sent outside the EEA (General Data Protection Regulation, 2016, art. 44-47).11 Our KeyCDN list does not prove that such a transfer happened, and it does not prove that it did not happen.

What each Webbkoll column means

Read Table 2 from left to right as one homepage load. Internal cookies: files set by the site itself. External cookies: files set by other parties. Third-party requests: how many times the page called another host. Countries excl. Norway: how many different countries those other hosts mapped to, after dropping Norway. The country list can still print NO if Norway appeared in the raw look-up; the count column already excludes it.

Table 2 is the first scan of all 18 sites on 20 August 2026. Use it with Figures 2 to 4. Table 3 is the same 18 sites later that day, with third-party countries looked up on 21 August 2026. Treat Table 3 as a check: a later visit can change. Do not treat it as a second ranking, and do not average it with Table 2.

Part 2: Does the site have a lawful reason for this use of data?

What the ICO tool is

The Information Commissioner's Office (ICO) is the UK data protection authority. It publishes a step-by-step tool that asks about one processing activity and then marks which Article 6 bases look APPROPRIATE (Information Commissioner's Office, 2026).14 We used the official Word report from that tool (dated 26 August 2026), not a screenshot of the questions. A controller must name a purpose first, then a basis for that purpose (Information Commissioner's Office, n.d.). A web site does not have one basis for everything it does. Tax data, optional cookies and a shop checkout are different purposes. Mix them in one run, and the tool mixes the answers. Each row in Table 5 is one purpose on one site from Table 1a. The tool is UK guidance used because the assignment asks for it. It does not replace a full audit.

Why a cookie banner is not Article 6

Article 6 answers: why may we use this personal data? Cookie and ePrivacy rules answer a different question: may we store or read information on this device (ePrivacy Directive, 2002)? A Yes/No banner can be the right test for optional analytics cookies. It is the wrong test for a tax file that the law requires the Tax Administration to keep. If we put tax and cookies in one ICO run, the tool will pick the wrong basis.

skatteetaten.no: tax data versus optional cookies

The Tax Administration notice says that processing of identity, income and tax data is required by law and is generally not optional (Norwegian Tax Administration, n.d.-a). The ICO report marks legal obligation and public task APPROPRIATE. Consent is NOT APPROPRIATE and likely invalid, because a person cannot give a genuine, free, ongoing No to a statutory duty.

Optional statistics cookies (Skyra, Matomo, Google Analytics, Siteimprove) are a different purpose. They use a Yes/No banner. A second ICO run marks consent INCONCLUSIVE: there is a choice, but the request does not fully name controller, purpose and types. No basis is APPROPRIATE for that cookie purpose. Table 5 therefore has two rows for the same site.

Webbkoll never clicks the banner (5th of July Foundation, n.d.-b). That is why Table 2 shows five internal cookies and zero external cookies for skatteetaten.no even though the notice still offers optional statistics cookies. The ICO cookie row tests the notice, not the Webbkoll count. On 24 August 2026 we opened skatteetaten.no/person/ in a clean browser and selected No. That only tests whether optional tracking is blocked after rejection. It is not the lawful basis for tax administration. Figure 7 shows the hub that already splits "privacy notice" and "cookies", which is the same split as Table 5.

netflix.no: paid streaming

The purpose is account and payment data needed to provide the paid subscription (Netflix, 2026). Contractual necessity means the processing is needed to perform the agreement, not merely useful for the company. ICO marks contract APPROPRIATE. Consent is likely invalid: there is no real, ongoing choice if the film cannot be delivered without the account. Ads, recommendations and marketing in the same notice are other purposes and were kept out of this run.

fotball.no: name and club on the match list

Norges Fotballforbund publishes name and club of active players aged 13+ as match history, with opt-out through the club (Norges Fotballforbund, n.d.). ICO marks legitimate interests APPROPRIATE after a necessity and balancing check. NFF is a sports federation, not a public authority, so public task is not the fit. Membership in the FIKS system may rest on contract.15 Cookiebot may rest on ePrivacy consent. Those are other purposes. A "samtykke" tick in club software is not automatically valid GDPR consent if the person has no real choice.

document.no: Google Signals

This is the case that fails the consent test. Google Signals is described as advertising analytics.16 Article 6 is not named (Document Media AS, n.d.). Collection is said to run only with a Google login and ad personalization. That looks like consent. ICO marks no basis APPROPRIATE. Consent is INCONCLUSIVE because a valid request must be clear, prominent, and separate from other terms. The real choice sits in Google settings, and Document Pluss terms also bundle acceptance of the notice. Contract and legitimate interests are not a fit for this advertising purpose. Table 5 marks the row Partial.

babyshop.no: checkout

The purpose is name, address, contact, order and payment data needed to deliver goods. The sales terms are a purchase contract (Babyshop Sthlm Holding AB, n.d.). ICO marks contract APPROPRIATE. The notice does not label Article 6(1)(b), but the activity is still checkout. Marketing and cookies stay separate. A bundled "I agree to cookies" inside the terms is not valid Article 6 consent.

Conclusion

Part 1 asked how much each of the 18 course sites contacts other hosts. Webbkoll shows that contact. fotball.no has the most third-party requests (113) and still sets no third-party cookies. document.no maps to the most countries. lanekassen.no and altinn.no are the quietest government fronts. News and media contact the most hosts as a sector. Government contacts the fewest.

Part 2 asked whether five chosen sites have a named, workable Article 6 basis for one purpose each. Four core purposes match the notice (tax, Netflix streaming, football match lists, babyshop checkout). Two consent tests fail: optional cookies on skatteetaten.no and Google Signals on document.no. A legal duty to assess tax is not a cookie click. One purpose needs one basis.

This is one week's snapshot, not a year of traffic. KeyCDN countries show where machines appeared to sit. They are not a GDPR Chapter V transfer record (General Data Protection Regulation, 2016, art. 44-47). A later week on the same 18 URLs, and one extra purpose per ICO site, would test whether the picture holds.
